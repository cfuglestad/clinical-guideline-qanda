SCREENING FOR HYPERTENSION IN ADULTS
US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement

SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATION

The USPSTF recommends screening for hypertension in adults aged 18 years or older with office blood pressure measurement. The USPSTF recommends obtaining blood pressure measurements outside of the clinical setting for diagnostic confirmation before starting treatment. This is a Grade A recommendation.

Hypertension is defined as persistent systolic blood pressure at or above 130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure at or above 80 mm Hg. These thresholds are based on the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline. Approximately 45 percent of US adults have hypertension by this definition.

RATIONALE

Importance. Hypertension is one of the most common conditions seen in primary care and is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, cognitive decline, and premature death. Uncontrolled hypertension contributes to approximately 500,000 deaths annually in the United States.

Detection. Office blood pressure measurement is the standard screening method. Automated oscillometric devices are preferred over manual auscultatory measurement for their improved accuracy and reduced observer bias. A single elevated reading should be confirmed with repeated measurements, ideally using ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) or home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) before diagnosis.

Benefits of Early Detection and Treatment. Treatment of hypertension with antihypertensive medications significantly reduces the risk of stroke (35-40 percent relative risk reduction), myocardial infarction (20-25 percent relative risk reduction), and heart failure (approximately 50 percent relative risk reduction). Treatment benefits extend across all age groups and racial/ethnic populations.

Harms of Screening and Treatment. The harms of screening are minimal and include potential anxiety from labeling and the inconvenience of follow-up visits. Treatment-related harms include medication adverse effects such as dizziness, electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury, though serious adverse events are uncommon with standard first-line agents.

CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS

1. Patient Population Under Consideration

This recommendation applies to adults aged 18 years or older who do not have known hypertension. It does not apply to children and adolescents younger than 18 years or to pregnant persons, for whom separate screening recommendations exist.

2. Assessment of Risk

Risk factors for hypertension include older age, Black race, family history, obesity, physical inactivity, high sodium diet, excessive alcohol consumption, and chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease. Adults with multiple risk factors should be screened more frequently.

3. Screening Intervals

The optimal screening interval has not been established in rigorous trials. Based on expert opinion and observational data, the USPSTF suggests the following intervals: annual screening for adults aged 40 years or older and for adults at increased risk; screening every 3 to 5 years for adults aged 18 to 39 years with normal blood pressure and no risk factors.

4. Confirmatory Blood Pressure Measurement

Before initiating treatment, elevated office blood pressure should be confirmed using out-of-office measurements. Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is considered the reference standard for confirming hypertension and can identify white coat hypertension and masked hypertension. Home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) is a reasonable alternative when ABPM is not available or not tolerated. A minimum of 12 to 14 measurements over several days is recommended for HBPM.

5. Treatment Approaches

First-line pharmacologic agents include thiazide diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), and calcium channel blockers. Selection should consider patient comorbidities, potential adverse effects, and cost. Lifestyle modifications including dietary changes (DASH diet), sodium reduction, weight loss, regular physical activity, and moderation of alcohol intake are recommended for all patients with elevated blood pressure.

EVIDENCE REVIEW

Scope. The USPSTF commissioned a systematic evidence review to update its 2015 recommendation on screening for high blood pressure in adults. The review examined evidence on the accuracy of screening methods, the benefits and harms of treatment at different blood pressure thresholds, and the effectiveness of confirmatory measurement strategies.

Accuracy of Screening. Office blood pressure measurement has moderate sensitivity (approximately 75 percent) and specificity (approximately 75 percent) for identifying true hypertension when compared with ambulatory monitoring. Repeated office measurements improve accuracy. Automated devices reduce white coat effects and produce readings closer to ambulatory measurements.

Benefits of Treatment. A meta-analysis of 48 randomized controlled trials including over 340,000 participants demonstrated that blood pressure lowering reduces major cardiovascular events by approximately 20 percent per 10 mm Hg reduction in systolic blood pressure. Benefits were consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, baseline cardiovascular risk, and comorbidities.

Harms of Treatment. Treatment with antihypertensive medications is associated with increased risk of hypotension (relative risk 1.7), syncope (relative risk 1.3), acute kidney injury (relative risk 1.2), and electrolyte abnormalities (relative risk 1.9). These harms are generally manageable with monitoring and dose adjustment. Intensive blood pressure targets (below 120/80 mm Hg) are associated with higher rates of adverse events compared with standard targets.

DISCUSSION

The evidence supports screening all adults for hypertension using office blood pressure measurement. Early detection and treatment of hypertension substantially reduces cardiovascular morbidity and mortality with acceptable harms. The net benefit of screening is substantial across all adult populations.

Current practice patterns suggest that most adults have their blood pressure measured at routine clinical encounters. However, a significant proportion of adults with hypertension remain undiagnosed or inadequately treated. Improving screening rates, ensuring confirmatory measurement before treatment, and addressing barriers to treatment adherence remain important public health priorities.

Research gaps include the optimal screening interval for different risk groups, the comparative effectiveness of different confirmatory measurement strategies in diverse populations, the benefits and harms of screening in young adults aged 18 to 39 years, and the role of emerging technologies such as cuffless blood pressure monitors.

RECOMMENDATIONS OF OTHER ORGANIZATIONS

The American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology recommend blood pressure screening at every healthcare encounter. The American Academy of Family Physicians endorses the USPSTF recommendation. The European Society of Hypertension recommends opportunistic screening for adults and targeted screening for high-risk populations. These organizations generally align on the importance of confirmatory measurement before diagnosis.
